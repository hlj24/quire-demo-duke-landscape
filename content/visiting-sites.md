Work began in early 1925 with a visit to Durham. Olmsted partner Percival Gallagher took the lead on the project. Gallagher had studied fine arts at Harvard and horticulture at the Bussey Institution, and he already had experience in planning campuses at Haverford, Swarthmore, and Vassar colleges. Olmsted, Jr., noted Gallagher's aptitude for "solving special problems," making him an ideal candidate for the task at hand.

{% figure '3men' %}

## Construction begins: the Woman's College

By the time Gallagher arrived on campus on February 25, 1925, preparations were already being made for the transformation of Trinity College into the Woman's College. Tracks had been laid for a spur from the nearby rail line to carry construction materials into the heart of campus, and perhaps most impressively, to move several entire buildings to Kittrell College, a historically black college north of Raleigh. 

{% figure 'train' %}

Gallagher also sought a view of the Woman's College from afar: what did the campus look like from further away? How might landscape design enhance that view?

## Traveling to the New University Site

Gallagher also ventured out by car to see the land that would become West Campus.

This view of Rigsbee Road (below), a portion of which would become Duke University Road, is looking toward the site for West Campus along the tree line at the top right.

{% figure 'RigsbeeRd1' %}

This land was a mixture of farms, woodland, and swamp that was accessible by a macadamized, or paved, road, with rough field roads cutting across the landscape. The telephone poles that line Rigsbee Road here indicate that new communications technologies were already making their presence known. These would become key infrastructure for the modern university.

{% figure 'RigsbeeRd2' %}

These lands were inhabited by farmers, tenant farmers, mill workers, and others. White landowners rented their lands to Black and white tenants. A few Black landowners ran their own businesses out of their homes or invested in their property as real estate. 

A key site for the university would be the Rigsbee farm, which would provide the bulk of the land for West Campus. The Rigsbees' farm house and barns were located on what would become part of the athletics facility.

{% figure 'RigsbeeFarm' %}

On the site where West Campus's dorms would be constructed was the home of an African American family who may have been tenants of the Rigsbees.

{% figure 'AfricanAmericanHome' %}

The land that Gallagher saw was full of ridges and valleys. These were important to observe because Gallagher was responsible for more than planting trees and flowers: his firm would help plan the grading needed to create level ground for the campus; they would consider how to use existing roads and where to build new roads; and they would plan modern infrastructure like electricity, water, and sewer lines. As he looked across the hills and valleys, he must have had all of these considerations in mind.

{% figure 'RuralDurham' %}

## The Woman's College Progresses

By Gallagher's 1927 visit, many of the Woman's College buildings had been constructed or were under construction. Although there were still tracks down the center quadrangle and building materials piled throughout the site, Gallagher could begin to envision what the landscape would look like once the campus had been completed.
